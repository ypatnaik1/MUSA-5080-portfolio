ctrl <- trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10)

cv_rmse <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  vars <- all.vars(formula)
  bad <- vars[sapply(vars, function(v) any(is.na(df[[v]])))]
  if (length(bad) > 0) {
    stop("These variables have NAs: ", paste(bad, collapse = ", "),
         ". Fill them or choose another variable (Rule 1: don't drop rows).")
  }
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm", trControl = ctrl)
  fit$results$RMSE
}

Part 3: Getting data from Philadelphia’s open data portal

Many of the city’s datasets on OpenDataPhilly are served through a Carto SQL API. You send a SQL query in a URL, and it sends back data. Ask for format=GeoJSON and st_read() reads the result directly, just like the Indego feed in Week 4.

The pattern:

https://phl.carto.com/api/v2/sql?format=GeoJSON&q=SELECT ... FROM table_name WHERE ...

Three pieces of SQL are all you need:

  • SELECT the_geom, column1, column2 chooses which columns come back. Always include the_geom, or you get no geometry.
  • FROM table_name chooses the dataset.
  • WHERE ... filters rows. Filter in the query, not in R. Some tables have millions of rows, and pulling everything will be slow or fail.

This helper builds the URL for you. Spaces and symbols in a URL need encoding, and URLencode() handles that.

Code 
carto_get <- function(sql) {
  url <- paste0("https://phl.carto.com/api/v2/sql?format=GeoJSON&q=", URLencode(sql))
  st_read(url, quiet = TRUE)
}

I chose SEPTA transit stops as well as land use because they can help explain differences found in the percentage of households without vehicles across Philadelphia. Transit stops capture access to public transportation, which may reduce the need to own a car. Land use captures how neighborhoods are developed, including residential, commercial, and mixed-use areas, which may influence walkability and access to nearby destinations. Unlike the ACS variables of income, population, and building age, these layers capture characteristics of the built environment and transportation accessibility that may help explain the spatial patterns in the baseline model’s residuals.

The spatial feature that helped the most was SEPTA stop density, which reduced the CV RMSE from 14.83589 in the baseline model to 12.76867 in m3. This suggests that the concentration of transit stops within a census tract is a useful predictor for the percentage of households without a vehicle. Distance to the nearest SEPTA stop also improved the model, reducing CV RMSE to 13.70125. However, the raw number of SEPTA stops per tract slightly worsened prediction, increasing CV RMSE to 14.91198. I thought this feature would help because areas with more transit stops might have more households that do not own vehicles. One possible explanation is that since census tracts vary in size, larger tracts may contain more stops without necessarily having better transit accessibility. Stop density accounts for tract area, which may explain why it performed better than the raw counts.

Reading the coefficients: R turns a factor with k categories into k − 1 dummy variables. The category left out is the reference level (here, “Core,” the first level). Each coefficient is the difference from the reference: ringOuter is how much higher or lower predicted Y is in outer tracts compared to core tracts, holding everything else in the model constant. To change the reference, use relevel(ring, ref = "Outer").

NoteCheck your group sizes

If a category has only a handful of tracts, its coefficient is unstable, and in cross-validation some folds may not contain it at all. Aim for at least 20 tracts per category.

Part 7: Reflection

The feature that improved the model the most was SEPTA stop density, which reduced CV RMSE from 14.83589 in the baseline model to 12.76867. This suggests that the concentration of transit stops is useful for predicting the percentage of households without vehicles in Philadelphia. Stop density likely performed better because it accounts for differences in census tract area, making comparisons between tracts more meaningful. In contrast, the raw number of SEPTA stops was the most disappointing feature, slightly increasing CV RMSE to 14.91198. Larger tracts may contain more transit stops without necessarily having better transit accessibility, which could explain why the raw count performed poorly. None of the transformations tested improved upon the original stop density model. In the final model, the coefficient for SEPTA stop density was 0.04988, indicating that each additional stop record per square mile is associated with a 0.04988 percentage-point increase in the percentage of households without vehicles, holding income, population, and building age constant. In terms of prediction, a tract with 10 additional stop records per square mile would be predicted to have approximately 0.50 percentage points more households without vehicles, assuming the other variables remain unchanged. This relationship suggests that transit availability may be an important factor in understanding differences in vehicle ownership across Philadelphia. The final residual map shows that the model continues to underestimate the percentage of households without vehicles in parts of South and central Philadelphia, while overestimating it in many northern and northeastern tracts. One additional factor worth examining is transit service frequency, since the number of stops alone does not capture how frequently buses or trains operate. SEPTA’s published transit schedules or service data could help measure this. Including service frequency could help distinguish neighborhoods with many stops but limited service from those with more frequent transit options. Finally, the uncertainty plot shows no consistent relationship between ACS estimate uncertainty and prediction error. Several large residuals occur in tracts with relatively low coefficients of variation, suggesting that statistical uncertainty may explain some remaining error, but missing geographic and transportation factors likely contribute as well.

ctrl <- trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10)

cv_rmse <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  vars <- all.vars(formula)
  bad <- vars[sapply(vars, function(v) any(is.na(df[[v]])))]
  if (length(bad) > 0) {
    stop("These variables have NAs: ", paste(bad, collapse = ", "),
         ". Fill them or choose another variable (Rule 1: don't drop rows).")
  }
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm", trControl = ctrl)
  fit$results$RMSE
}

## Part 3: Getting data from Philadelphia's open data portal

Many of the city's datasets on [OpenDataPhilly](https://opendataphilly.org) are served through a **Carto SQL API**. You send a SQL query in a URL, and it sends back data. Ask for `format=GeoJSON` and `st_read()` reads the result directly, just like the Indego feed in Week 4.

The pattern:

```
https://phl.carto.com/api/v2/sql?format=GeoJSON&q=SELECT ... FROM table_name WHERE ...
```

Three pieces of SQL are all you need:

- `SELECT the_geom, column1, column2` chooses which columns come back. **Always include `the_geom`**, or you get no geometry.
- `FROM table_name` chooses the dataset.
- `WHERE ...` filters rows. **Filter in the query, not in R.** Some tables have millions of rows, and pulling everything will be slow or fail.

This helper builds the URL for you. Spaces and symbols in a URL need encoding, and `URLencode()` handles that.

```{r}
#| label: carto-helper
carto_get <- function(sql) {
  url <- paste0("https://phl.carto.com/api/v2/sql?format=GeoJSON&q=", URLencode(sql))
  st_read(url, quiet = TRUE)
}
```

### Finding a table name

On a dataset's OpenDataPhilly page, look for the API link. The table name is the word after `FROM` in the example query. To see what values a column takes before you filter on it, ask for a count in CSV format:

**Reading the coefficients:** R turns a factor with k categories into k − 1 dummy variables. The category left out is the **reference level** (here, "Core," the first level). Each coefficient is the **difference from the reference**: `ringOuter` is how much higher or lower predicted Y is in outer tracts *compared to core tracts*, holding everything else in the model constant. To change the reference, use `relevel(ring, ref = "Outer")`.

::: {.callout-note}
## Check your group sizes
If a category has only a handful of tracts, its coefficient is unstable, and in cross-validation some folds may not contain it at all. Aim for at least 20 tracts per category.
:::

## Part 7: Reflection 

The feature that improved the model the most was SEPTA stop density, which reduced CV RMSE from 14.83589 in the baseline model to 12.76867. This suggests that the concentration of transit stops is useful for predicting the percentage of households without vehicles in Philadelphia. Stop density likely performed better because it accounts for differences in census tract area, making comparisons between tracts more meaningful. In contrast, the raw number of SEPTA stops was the most disappointing feature, slightly increasing CV RMSE to 14.91198. Larger tracts may contain more transit stops without necessarily having better transit accessibility, which could explain why the raw count performed poorly. None of the transformations tested improved upon the original stop density model. In the final model, the coefficient for SEPTA stop density was 0.04988, indicating that each additional stop record per square mile is associated with a 0.04988 percentage-point increase in the percentage of households without vehicles, holding income, population, and building age constant. In terms of prediction, a tract with 10 additional stop records per square mile would be predicted to have approximately 0.50 percentage points more households without vehicles, assuming the other variables remain unchanged. This relationship suggests that transit availability may be an important factor in understanding differences in vehicle ownership across Philadelphia. The final residual map shows that the model continues to underestimate the percentage of households without vehicles in parts of South and central Philadelphia, while overestimating it in many northern and northeastern tracts. One additional factor worth examining is transit service frequency, since the number of stops alone does not capture how frequently buses or trains operate. SEPTA's published transit schedules or service data could help measure this. Including service frequency could help distinguish neighborhoods with many stops but limited service from those with more frequent transit options. Finally, the uncertainty plot shows no consistent relationship between ACS estimate uncertainty and prediction error. Several large residuals occur in tracts with relatively low coefficients of variation, suggesting that statistical uncertainty may explain some remaining error, but missing geographic and transportation factors likely contribute as well.
